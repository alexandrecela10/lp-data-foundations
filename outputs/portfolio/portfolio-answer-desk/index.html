01 · VISION

Every question from the CIO gets the right answer, fast.

Today each question means hours in reports, letters and spreadsheets, and the answer depends on who searched. The vision: one number whoever answers, with the source and date of each figure, in minutes.

02 · THE PROBLEM

Three things go wrong today.

1 · A wrong total on a company in the news

You own the same company through several funds, under several spellings. Search one name and you miss part of it, or a write-down sent only in a letter.

2 · A committee pack that doesn't match last quarter

Each analyst calculates returns their own way. Nobody can say why the numbers changed.

3 · A manager decision without the full picture

Fee changes and partners leaving are spread across years of letters. Nobody reads them all.

03 · THE SOLUTION

The AI reads the question. Code does the sums. You send.

What it is

A question desk for an LP's private equity team: a chat, a committee pack and a manager note, on one set of agreed definitions.

What it does

You ask in plain English. The AI picks the measure, the company and the date. Code calculates the answer and links each figure to its document.

What you get

A number you can send to the CIO before the meeting, the same one whoever asks.

How it's built input, steps, output, and how it was tested

Input: six made-up sources, each arriving on its own schedule: holdings (daily), notices of cash called and paid out (about weekly), fund reports (quarterly, 40 to 90 days late, in four layouts), manager letters, reference data, and news every 15 minutes.

Steps: documents are kept as received (bronze). They are read into checked fields, and bad rows are stopped (silver: 26 broken notices stopped). Then they become tables where every row names its source (gold). The AI turns the question into fixed fields; the semantic layer calculates the number; the graph follows ownership and people links; a checker blocks any number the code didn't calculate.

Output: a chat answer, a committee dashboard with 3 views and export, a manager note, and a read-only query notebook for the data team.

Test: 60 questions with known answers, five setups. Scored first on whether the answer is right, then on time to an answer you can send, using stated checking times. A second made-up portfolio, with new spellings, scored separately.

Terms on this page plain definitions
  • LP: an investor, such as a pension fund, that puts money into private equity funds.
  • Fund of funds: a fund that invests in other funds.
  • Co-investment: a deal where the LP invests in a company directly, next to a fund.
  • Stake bought from another investor (secondary): a share of a fund bought second-hand.
  • Exposure: our share of a company's value, added up over every way we own it.
  • Semantic layer: a list of measures, each defined once with an owner, that every tool uses.
  • Identity table: a list that links every spelling of a company to one record.
  • Graph: things plus the links between them; a question becomes a walk along the links.
  • As of a past date: answering with only what we knew on that date.
  • IRR and TVPI: two return measures. TVPI is cash back plus current value, divided by cash paid in.

Portfolio Answer Desk: Opportunity Analysis

Date: 2026-10-25 (revised the same day: vision added, commercial value shortened and sized) Owner: PM (drafted by Claude, run unattended with PM approval) Status: Go

Vision

Every data request from the CIO gets the right answer, fast: one number whoever answers, with the source, definition and date of each figure, in minutes.

This project moves toward that in two ways: - Proof. It measures which way of organising the data gets an analyst to a correct, sourced answer fastest, on 60 questions with known answers. - Teaching. It explains the building blocks behind it in plain words, so a data leader can pick them knowingly.

Problem Statement

Senior leaders at a large LP ask unplanned questions about the portfolio. An LP is an investor, such as a pension fund, that commits money to many private equity funds. Each answer takes an analyst hours to a day. Two analysts can return two different numbers for the same question.

Current Situation

Sara, a portfolio analyst, answers by hand. She searches fund reports, manager letters and spreadsheets, then adds up what she finds.

A concrete case (hypothetical, synthetic data). Monday 08:10, the news says Calder Packaging is restructuring its debt. At 08:12 the CIO asks: "How exposed are we, through which managers, and did we know?" - We hold the company 5 ways: through a fund we invested in, twice through a fund that invests in other funds, through a direct investment alongside a manager (a co-investment), and through a fund stake we bought from another investor. - The company appears under three spellings across those reports. - A drop in its value appears only in a manager letter dated 10 September.

Sara finds the first holding in minutes. The other four and the letter take the rest of the morning, and she may still miss one. Had the company sat in one fund under one name, she'd have answered in five minutes.

Proposed Solution

One shared, organised set of portfolio data, with a different screen for each of the three kinds of request:

  • The CIO's question about a company in the news: a chat assistant. An AI reads the question and picks which agreed calculation to run. Code does the maths. Every number shows its source and the date it was true.
  • The investment committee pack: a dashboard where each measure (for example a fund's return) has one written definition, one owner and a version number, computed as of a set date.
  • A decision on whether to invest in a manager's next fund: a briefing note with the numbers, plus what the manager's letters said, each line citing its letter.

Code checks the AI's work. The AI can only pick calculations that exist. It refuses when no agreed definition exists, or when the person asking isn't allowed to see the data.

Solution Overview

Five setups share one synthetic world. Each request gets the screen that suits it.

C+ids exists to test whether the graph earns its place on ownership, not just on spellings. Offline, A and B get help so they aren't easy to beat: A's search also tries each company's other names; B gets prepared tables with column notes and parent links.

Screens: chat (request 1), a dashboard with 3 views (request 2), a briefing note (request 3), and a read-only SQL page for the data team.

States designed: - Refusal card: what was asked, why it was refused, who can answer, and "Answer what I can see instead". - Waiting item: "1 letter item not counted, waiting for review" under any total it affects. - Empty card for a company we don't hold, and a banner when the offline stand-in answered. - Dates: every holding shows its own date. A source older than 90 days reads "stale". The first line says "as of mixed dates" when dates differ. - Copy answer to send copies the paragraph, holdings, sources, dates, Sara's note and the disclaimer. - Every screen, copy and export carries "Synthetic data. AI Behavior Contract

Topic Rule
AI's jobs Send the question to the right path; turn the question into a calculation request; write the answer text around numbers code computed
What it sees The question, the asker's role, and one of: the calculation menu (C, D), the table list (B), retrieved passages (A)
Numbers Every number comes from code or a cited passage, inserted into the text by placeholders. A checker reads every number in the final text and refuses the answer if one wasn't computed. The AI never does arithmetic
Access Each role sees its own copy of the tables; search drops documents the role can't see. The role picker is a demo switch, not a login
Dates Every event has two dates: when it took effect and when we learnt of it. "As of D" counts only what we learnt on or before D
Hosted demo Offline only, no key. Live calls only on a local run with a key, with the notice "Questions go to Anthropic. Do not enter real data."
Never Numbers from news; numbers with no source; a calculation with no agreed definition; data the role can't see; real organisation names
Model claude-sonnet-5-5 when ANTHROPIC_API_KEY is set; an offline stand-in otherwise
Speed, one answer Live: half of answers under 10 s, 95% under 30 s. Offline: under 1 s

If something fails: a live call fails, so the offline stand-in answers and says so. A letter event with low confidence shows as "waiting for review" and isn't counted.

How we test it: - 60 questions, 20 per request, with answers known from the data generator, traps included. - Definitions written first, in docs/DEFINITIONS.md. The code that computes the right answers reads the generator only, never the setups. - A second, held-out world (another seed, extra spelling noise the pipeline was never tuned on) is scored separately. - The pytest gate guards the build (pipeline, access, disclaimer, number checker). It doesn't rank the setups; the live run does.

Appendix: challenges to the brief (written down, as the brief asks)

  1. The main metric depends on an assumed checking time. A setup that shows sources wins partly by design. We publish a sensitivity table and report correctness on its own.
  2. Offline A and B use fixed rules in place of an AI. They show each setup's limits, not Claude's error rate. Live results are a separate, labelled run.
  3. "A graph for company identity" mixes two jobs. Matching spellings is a lookup an identity table can also do. We test C+ids, so the graph has to earn its place on ownership, not only spellings.
  4. 20 questions per request is small. One question moves a score by 5 points. We report counts, not only percentages.
  5. The forecast and plan figures are rules, not truth. For the cash call forecast, "correct" means "matches the agreed rule as of the date". That tests consistency, not foresight.
