01 · VISION

See every sale first. Price it best. Prove every number.

Today an adviser sends the same short offer to many buyers, and everyone prices it from numbers that are months old. You already invest in some of those funds, so you know more. The vision: you hear of sales early, and every price you offer uses everything you know.

02 · THE PROBLEM

Four things go wrong today.

1 · A day lost on offers you'll pass on

Offers hide fund names until you sign a confidentiality agreement. You work out each fund by hand.

2 · You pay what everyone pays, though you know more

The offer is priced from the funds' value in June. Your own reports since then show sales and write-downs. That news rarely reaches your first price.

3 · Same offer, different answers

What a good deal looks like lives in policy papers. Each analyst applies it in their head.

4 · You hear of a sale with everyone else

Pensions often hint at a sale in the news first. Nobody reads it every week.

03 · THE SOLUTION

The AI reads. Code checks. You decide.

What it is

Two assistants for a secondaries team. Offer Check triages secondaries opportunities. Seller Watch proactively finds secondaries before they come to market.

What it does

Offer Check reads a sale offer, works out which funds are in it, and checks the price against your own fund reports. Seller Watch reads the news every 15 minutes and, each Monday, lists pensions showing signs they may sell.

What you get

Faster, better-informed decisions on which offers to take to the NDA stage. Candidate opportunities, found before the competition. Every number and news item shows its source.

06 · LIMITATIONS

What this doesn't prove yet.

  • Data: your funds, the offers and the fund news are made up. The price test is circular: one program made both the news and the later values.
  • Answers: the correct answers were written by the builder or an AI. A real test needs 2 or 3 analysts.
  • Users: no user tests. Time saved is unobserved.
  • Seller Watch: 2 past sales, no early warning on either.
  • Emails: all made by the same program. Real offers written by people may read differently.
07 · SCOPE AND NEXT STEPS

What it won't do, and what comes next.

Non-goals
  • A final bid price. That comes after the confidentiality agreement.
  • A price for funds you don't own. You have no reports on them.
  • A briefing on each pension's holdings. Needs real holdings data.
  • Contacting sellers. The partner makes the call.
Next
  1. Test the price check on past offers and past fund reports.
  2. Test on offers written by people.
  3. Ask 2 or 3 analysts to give their own answers, and time them.
  4. Let one confident sale report count on its own, then replay more past sales.
  5. Use an AI model hosted in your region that keeps no data, before any real offer.
RESOURCES

Go deeper.

Main learnings a rule fix that made things worse, batch beat streaming, and no early warning

A rule fix that made things worse

Version 2 of the rules changed one threshold to fix one offer. It then agreed with 14 of the 15 offers used to write it, but only 10 of the 14 test offers it had never seen. Version 1 got 13 of 14. The rules had learnt the examples, not the job. Version 3 judged price separately, with your own fund data: 14 of 14. Supports hypothesis 3.

Price needs its own question

In version 2, one offer looked like a fit only because its low price lifted its score. Asking "does it fit us?" and "is the price right?" separately made both answers clear. Supports hypotheses 2 and 3.

Batch beat streaming, twice

Each tool was built two ways: handling each item the moment it arrives (streaming), or collecting items on a schedule (batch). Both gave the same results and recovered from a crash without losing or double-counting anything. Batch won. The news only updates every 15 minutes, and decisions take days. Streaming needed 3 parts running all month; batch needs 1, running about 11 hours a month. The comparison also caught a real bug.

Claude vs simple rules

On 29 offers, Claude and the offline rules did equally well. Claude took 5.6 seconds per offer; the rules took a quarter of a second. On news headlines, Claude caught 21 of 23 real sale signs, the rules 19.

No early warning, and why

One fresh report of a sale, on its own, scores 0.57, just under the 0.6 line. In the New York City case, a correct report 12 weeks before the sale stayed below the line. So Seller Watch stays a monitor. Hypothesis 4 is not supported yet.

Secondaries Radar: Opportunity Analysis

Date: 2026-10-24 (price check added 2026-10-25) Owner: Alexandre Cela Status: Draft (recommendation: Go)

Vision

See every sale first. Price it best. Prove every number.

The fund buys private equity stakes from investors who want out early. The end: buy more of the right stakes, at lower prices, without additional headcount.

This opportunity delivers the first two steps: - Seller Watch (Gate 0): spot investors likely to sell, before a sale starts. - Offer Check (Gate 1): decide in under an hour whether a sale offer deserves a closer look, and whether its price is right by what we already know.

Problem Statement

The fund hears about most sales late, when a broker sends a short sale summary to many buyers at once. Each summary then takes about a day of analyst work before anyone can say "look closer" or "pass". And we price it from the same out-of-date numbers as every other buyer, even when our own records say more.

Current Situation

  • Late: signs that an investor wants to sell often appear first in public, in board papers and the press. Nobody reads these sources systematically.
  • Slow: sale summaries hide fund names behind abbreviations. An analyst matches them by hand, then checks the fund's own records on each one.
  • Same numbers as everyone: brokers price every fund from its value on a past date, often 3 to 6 months old. Where we already invest in the same fund, we know what happened since: companies sold above or below their value, write-downs. That knowledge rarely makes it into the first view of the price.

Two concrete cases. - Late (public reporting). Washington State's pension board stopped a private equity sale in 2022, then restarted one with an advisor in 2023. A buyer reading its board papers knew the seller and the motive months before the second sale began. - Slow (labelled hypothetical). A broker sends "Project Cedar": stakes in 12 funds worth $340M, with names like "KPF IV". An analyst spends most of a day working out which funds these are. The fund already knows 9 of the 12 fund managers, but that only becomes clear on day two. It also invests in 3 of the funds, and one of them sold its largest company in August for 55% more than its value in June, the date the broker prices from. Nobody connects the two before the bid.

Proposed Solution

  • Seller Watch: a language model, an AI that reads text, flags news and board papers that suggest an investor may sell. Each flag quotes its source with a link. It gives a verdict (prepare, or watch), so the team can get ready weeks before an offer arrives. A partner decides what to do. The system never contacts a seller.
  • Offer Check: the language model reads each sale summary and matches every line to a known fund, with a confidence score. Sale offers hide fund names before an NDA, so the system identifies each fund from its numbers (share of commitment paid in, cash returned, total value), which are the same for every investor in a fund. Tested calculations then give a verdict: look closer, or pass.
  • Price check: for funds we already invest in, plain code starts from the broker's value, adds what we have learned since from our own fund reports, and shows what the stake is worth today by our data, next to the asking price. Each adjustment shows its source. The verdict then answers two questions: does it fit us, and is the price right.
  • Every verdict opens up: each verdict carries a confidence level and the reasons behind it, each reason tied to a rule and a number.
  • The team's rulebook: the team writes down, in plain text, what a good deal looks like for them: fund types, maximum price, minimum share of funds we already know. The AI applies these rules, not its own. The team can edit them at any time.
  • Human override: an analyst can overrule any verdict with a one-line reason. Every override is kept, and shows where the rules or the AI need to change.
  • Checks on the AI: the model can only use tested calculations, never invent a number. Every number shows its source. A test set of questions with known answers checks this.

Secondaries Radar: Offer Check and Seller Watch

Stage: Solution Review (demo build), v3: price check added Last Updated: 2026-10-25 Owner: Alexandre Cela Status: Approved for build Opportunity analysis: outputs/opportunity-analyses/secondaries-radar-opportunity.md (Go) Review: outputs/prds/secondaries-radar-solution-review-review-synthesis.md

1. What we're building

A working demo for an investor that buys private equity stakes from investors who want out early.

  • Offer Check (full build): reads a broker's sale offer as it arrives by email, identifies which funds it contains from their numbers (names are hidden before an NDA), and gives a verdict with plain-language reasons in minutes. The verdict answers two questions: does this offer fit us, and is the price right, judged by what we know as an investor in the same funds.
  • Seller Watch (thin build): reads real news as it is published, flags pensions showing signs they may sell, and quotes the evidence. The promise is preparation: we know who may sell weeks before the offer arrives, so our first bid is our best one.
  • Both: the team sets the rules for a good deal and can overrule any verdict. The AI never invents a number and never contacts anyone.
  • Engineering experiment: each feature gets a real-time and a batch collection path. Objective criteria pick one (section 9).

Users: an analyst (Offer Check, daily) and a partner (Seller Watch, weekly).

ID Requirement
D-1 Synthetic, our side: about 40 managers, 120 funds, quarterly series per fund (called, distributed, value), our holdings, sale offers and rulebook. Seeded and reproducible. Invented names only.
D-2 Real, Seller Watch only: news headlines from Google News RSS and the GDELT index. Store headline, link, source, publish time and a quote of 300 characters or less. A public list of about 50 US public pensions for linking.
D-3 No join of real and synthetic data. Seller Watch never compares real pensions with our synthetic portfolio, so no statement about a real firm's holdings is ever produced.
D-4 Layers: bronze (as received), silver (typed, validated, deduplicated), gold (metrics). A failed schema check blocks the row and logs it. Bronze stays out of git.
D-5 Point in time: a metric at date T uses only data reported on or before T. Every view shows its as-of date. The price check deliberately uses data up to the day the offer arrived, after the broker's reference date: that gap is the edge.
D-6 Synthetic, since the reference date: for funds we own, events reported to us after the offer's reference date: a company sold (value in June, sale value), a write-down (old value, new value), each with date, our share and a short note as it would appear in a fund report. Plus the fund's next quarterly value, for testing only, never shown to the price check.

5.2 Offer Check

ID Requirement
Q-1 Intake: offers arrive as emails (body text or a CSV/XLSX attachment). PDF is a stretch goal.
Q-2 Parse: the language model extracts offer-level fields (code name, seller type, reference date, currency, total value, asking price as % of value, bid deadline) and one row per fund line: strategy, region, vintage, fund size band, commitment, % called, DPI, TVPI, value, unfunded, name fragment if any. Output is schema-checked.
Q-3 Identify funds by fingerprint, in code, not by the model. Step 1: filter our fund universe by vintage, strategy and region. Step 2: for funds we own, compare % called, DPI, TVPI and value ÷ commitment at the offer's reference date. Tolerances: ±2 points on % called, ±0.05x on multiples. A name fragment adds to the score.
Q-4 Match confidence = 1 minus the mean normalised distance across available ratios, 0 to 1. Tiers: same fund (ratio match at 0.8 or more), same manager, other fund (description match only), unknown. Matches under 0.8 show up to 2 candidates. No silent match.
Q-5 Metrics (tested, versioned): share of value per tier, fit to plan, top-5 concentration, our exposure per manager, price range for same-fund lines from our own latest reported value (method shown; other tiers show "no price"). Currencies from a synthetic rate table at the reference date.
Q-6 Verdict: LOOK CLOSER or PASS, built from two parts: fit (rulebook, 5.4) and price (Q-8). Both show in the headline: "LOOK CLOSER: fits us, price looks cheap by our data."
Q-8 Price check, funds we own only. Worth today = broker's value + our share of each value change reported since the reference date (sale value minus June value; new value minus old value). Cash paid in or out since the reference date is shown apart, as an adjustment at closing, not a change in worth. Our price range = the existing band (Q-5) applied to worth today. Price view: CHEAP if the ask is at or below the top of our range, EXPENSIVE if above it, NO VIEW if no fund we own. Shown with coverage ("covers 41% of value") and every adjustment with its source row.
Q-9 Unidentified lines appear inside the reasons, not as a separate step: "Fund 9 (9% of value): not identified. Two funds we track fit: A or B. Know which?" A pick is recorded with the user, and the system's answer is kept next to it.
Q-7 Brief: one-page HTML within 2 minutes of the email arriving. Headline sentence first, then reasons, then lines. Each reason expands to its rule, number and source row. Message to the head of team: decision and ask first, then 3 to 5 plain bullets (who sells what and when, what we know, price, what is missing), then confidence in words. No scores, thresholds, rule ids or version numbers. Marked internal: it contains information from our own fund reports.

5.3 Seller Watch

ID Requirement
F-1 Collect real news continuously (real-time path) and on a schedule (batch path). Respect GDELT's limit of one request per 5 seconds.
F-2 Classify each item: allocation above target, liquidity need, policy or allocation review, leadership change, sale or advisor mentioned, or none. Link to one pension from the list, or none. Keep the quote.
F-3 Pension score = 1 − e^(−S), where S = sum of signal weight × classifier confidence × 0.5^(age in days / 90). Weights from the rulebook.
F-4 Verdict per pension: PREPARE (score 0.6 or more) or WATCH. Worded as our action, never as a claim about the pension.
F-5 Worked cases: replay 2 historical cases (for example Washington State 2022 to 2023) from frozen items, and show the timeline: first non-sale signal, PREPARE date, first sale report.

5.4 Rulebook, verdict, confidence, override

ID Requirement
R-1 Rulebook is a versioned YAML file with a plain-English description per rule. A command prints it as sentences for partners.
R-2 Price is not a fit rule; it is judged by Q-8. A price veto in the rulebook turns the verdict to PASS when the ask is above the top of our range by more than a set gap (default 10 points: before an NDA the guidance is indicative, so only a wide gap is worth walking away from). Score = weighted share of rules met. A rule whose metric can't be computed is excluded and listed as "not checked". Verdict positive if score ≥ threshold (default 0.6) and no veto rule fails.
R-3 Confidence shown in words first, then two parts: data matched (share of value matched at 0.8 or more, or confidently unknown) and margin (absolute gap between score and threshold; a veto counts as clear). Band: high if both are strong, low if either is weak, medium otherwise.
R-4 Reasons: veto rules first, then by weight. Each line: met / not met / not checked, number, threshold, source.
R-5 Override via CLI (radar override <offer> pass --reason "..."). Reason required. Stored with user, time, rulebook version and original verdict. Recorded, not learned from, in v1.
R-6 A rulebook change runs against the 8 labelled offers first. The diff is printed as sentences ("Cedar flips to PASS").

5.5 Checks on the AI

ID Requirement
C-1 The model only parses and classifies. All numbers in briefs come from tested functions.
C-2 Every number in a brief carries (table, row id, metric, version). A validator normalises numbers (68% = 0.68) and allows years and fund ordinals; any other unsourced number fails the brief.
C-3 Evals in pytest: metric fixtures (100% correct), labelled offer lines (match accuracy 90% or more), 50 labelled real headlines (classifier precision 80% or more), injection cases (0 rule changes).
C-4 Model claude-sonnet-5-5, pinned with prompt versions. Without an API key, a deterministic parser and keyword classifier run instead, and the brief says so.

8. Out of scope

  • Detailed pricing after an NDA (Gate 2): a bid from projected cash flows and a target return; needs data-room documents.
  • Price view for funds we don't own: we have no extra information on them.
  • Reading fund reports with the model: in the demo, events are stored as structured rows. Extracting them from real fund letters is v2.
  • Call briefing with pension holdings and price: needs a join of real holdings with our data; v2 with pseudonymised managers.
  • Identifying the seller from public holdings: v2.
  • Manager consent tracking, sending any message, learning from overrides, benchmarks (PME, quartiles): reasons as in v1.
  • Inbox integration with a real mail server, Excel export, CRM: v2.
  • Production: login, multi-user, hosting, UI polish.

9. Collection architecture experiment

Each feature builds both paths over the same input and the same processing code.

Feature Real-time path Batch path
Offer Check Local mail server receives an email and emits an event at once A job checks the mailbox every N minutes
Seller Watch Poller publishes each new article to a stream (Redpanda, or a local log if Docker is down); a consumer classifies continuously A job fetches and classifies every N minutes

Objective criteria, measured: 1. Freshness need: p95 latency must be under 10% of the decision window (Offer Check: 1 working day; Seller Watch: 4 weeks). 2. Correctness parity: both paths produce identical outputs on the same replay. 3. Recovery: kill mid-run, restart: 0 duplicates, 0 missed. 4. Moving parts: processes and services to run. 5. Code size: lines specific to the path. 6. Idle cost: always-on hours per month. 7. Replay time: rebuild all outputs from bronze.

Rule: choose the simplest path that passes 1 to 3. Ties go to fewer moving parts. Each result is filed in outputs/decisions/.

12. Decisions and open questions

  • D1 (closed): no pseudonymisation needed, because real and synthetic data never join (D-3).
  • D2: confidence uses fixed, visible rules, not model judgement.
  • D3: the model parses; code matches. Matching is auditable and testable.
  • D4: Seller Watch promises preparation, not exclusive access.
  • D5: price is a separate question from fit, decided by our own data on funds we own, with a team-set veto.
